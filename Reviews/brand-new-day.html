Spider-Man: Brand New Day

Finally Not Afraid of Its Own Continuity

Superhero/Action ‧ 140 minutes (approx.) ‧ PG-13 ‧ 2026 ‧ 4 min read

Rating ★★★★☆ 4 / 5

So many superhero franchise films become afraid of their own continuity the moment it gets complicated. Spider-Man: Brand New Day does the exact opposite. Directed by Destin Daniel Cretton, the 2026 film continues Tom Holland's run as Peter Parker following the memory-erasing spell at the end of No Way Home, and it's the MCU Spider-Man film fans have wanted ever since the character debuted back in 2016 - one that embraces everything this version of Peter Parker has experienced over the past decade and uses it as the foundation for the story instead of trying to wipe the slate clean for a “true” Spider-Man.

What makes the film so satisfying is how deliberately it seems to work through the shortcomings of the previous MCU Spider-Man films, almost as if someone sat down with a checklist and fixed them one by one. Nothing feels like a cynical retcon or an apology for what came before. Instead, every correction grows organically out of the continuity that already existed.

Aunt May is the clearest example. She finally feels like an essential part of Peter's life instead of a strangely distant supporting character. The film gives her warmth, emotional weight, and a genuine influence over Peter's decisions, remembering that Spider-Man stories are ultimately about Peter Parker's humanity.

The fallout from the memory spell is handled with the same care. The film never treats Peter being forgotten as a temporary inconvenience that exists only to be reversed. MJ remembers that Spider-Man mattered to her, but every piece of Peter Parker has been stripped away, leaving behind emotions she cannot fully understand. The film resists the obvious temptation to reunite them immediately, and the result is a relationship built on absence instead of nostalgia.

This is also the first time Peter is allowed to stand on his own without constantly being defined by another hero's legacy. There is no endless hero worship and no feeling that Spider-Man only matters because he is connected to a larger MCU universe. This is Peter's story; other heroes are simply here.

If there is one major weakness, it is the villain. Sadie Sink's character never quite clicks, coming across as more needlessly cruel than genuinely sympathetic.

Brand New Day feels like a film made by people who genuinely understood where this version of Spider-Man had stumbled and wanted to build on its strengths instead of pretending they never existed. It refuses to erase its own history, and because of that, it finally feels like a fully realized MCU Spider-Man. Spider-Man: Brand New Day gets a 4 out of 5.

(It embraces every scar this Peter has earned instead of pretending they never happened.)
